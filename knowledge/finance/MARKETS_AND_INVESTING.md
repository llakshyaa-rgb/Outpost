# Global Markets and Investing — Offline Field Guide


**Search terms:** stocks, shares, stock market, investing, investment, bonds, ETFs, funds, indices, valuation, P/E ratio, portfolio, trading, risk, global markets.

**Scope:** worldwide financial-market education. This is a learning reference, not investment advice, a recommendation, or a source of live prices. Rules, taxes, products and investor protections vary by jurisdiction.

## 1. What financial markets do
Financial markets connect people and institutions that need capital with those willing to provide it or take risk. Primary markets issue new securities; secondary markets allow existing securities to trade. Exchanges, brokers, dealers, clearing houses, custodians, regulators and central banks have different roles. A market price is the price at which a trade can currently clear, not a guarantee of intrinsic value.

## 2. Stocks (equities)
A share represents a residual ownership claim in a company. Shareholders may benefit from price appreciation and dividends, but can lose some or all invested capital. Common shares usually carry voting rights; preferred shares can have different dividend, liquidation and voting terms. A company's share count, share classes, dilution, buybacks, corporate actions and governance matter.

### How a stock trade works
An investor normally places an order through a broker. An exchange or other trading venue matches compatible buy and sell orders. A market order prioritises execution but not a specific price. A limit order sets the worst acceptable purchase price or minimum sale price but may never execute. Stop orders, stop-limit orders, auction mechanisms, short selling, margin and settlement rules differ across markets. Learn the venue's order rules before using them.

### Reading a company
Start with the business model, customers, products, competition, suppliers, management, governance, debt, cash generation, risks and industry structure. Read audited annual reports, interim reports, material-event filings, prospectuses and exchange disclosures. Distinguish reported facts, management guidance, analyst estimates and your own assumptions.

### Core valuation measures
- Market capitalisation = share price × shares outstanding.
- Enterprise value (EV) is commonly approximated as market cap + debt + preferred equity + non-controlling interests − cash and cash equivalents.
- P/E = share price / earnings per share. It is difficult to interpret for loss-making or unusually cyclical companies.
- P/S = market cap / revenue; useful with caution when earnings are negative.
- P/B = market cap / book equity; accounting quality and asset type matter.
- EV/EBITDA compares enterprise value with earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; it is not free cash flow.
- Dividend yield = annual dividend per share / share price. Dividends can be reduced or cancelled.
- Free cash flow is often approximated as operating cash flow − capital expenditure. Definitions vary.
No single ratio establishes that a security is cheap, safe or suitable. Compare like businesses, periods and accounting definitions.

## 3. Bonds and fixed income
A bond is debt issued by a government, municipality, company or other entity. Key terms include face value, coupon, maturity, yield, seniority, collateral, covenants, callability and credit rating. Bond prices generally move inversely to market yields. Risks include default, interest-rate duration, inflation, reinvestment, liquidity and currency risk. A rating is an opinion, not a guarantee.

## 4. Funds and pooled products
Mutual funds, exchange-traded funds (ETFs), index funds, money-market funds and closed-end funds have different structures. Examine the mandate, holdings, benchmark, concentration, tracking difference, fees, liquidity, spread, premium/discount, leverage, securities lending, domicile and tax treatment. An ETF is a trading wrapper, not automatically diversified or low-risk.

## 5. Other asset classes and markets
- Foreign exchange (FX): currency pairs, spot, forwards, swaps and options; exposed to interest-rate, policy, liquidity and geopolitical changes.
- Commodities: energy, metals and agriculture; spot and futures prices can differ because of storage, financing, seasonality and convenience yield.
- Derivatives: futures, forwards, options and swaps derive value from an underlying variable. Leverage, margin, expiry, volatility and counterparty risk can create losses larger or faster than expected.
- Real estate and REITs: property ownership or financing exposure; assess leverage, occupancy, rents, rates, geography and liquidity.
- Private equity and venture capital: less liquid investments with valuation, governance, lock-up and information risks.
- Digital assets: crypto tokens and related instruments involve custody, protocol, market, legal, fraud and extreme-volatility risks. Not all tokens represent ownership or claims on cash flows.

## 6. Fundamental and quantitative analysis
Fundamental analysis studies business quality, financial statements, competitive position and valuation. Technical analysis studies price, volume, trends and market behaviour; chart patterns are not guarantees. Quantitative research uses defined datasets, hypotheses, statistical tests and reproducible code. Beware look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, overfitting, data snooping, transaction costs and regime changes. Backtests are not proof of future performance.

## 7. Financial statements
- Income statement: revenue, expenses, operating profit, interest, tax and net income over a period.
- Balance sheet: assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time.
- Cash-flow statement: operating, investing and financing cash flows.
- Notes: accounting policies, segment data, debt maturities, related parties, commitments and contingencies.
Profit is not cash. Compare multiple years, reconcile non-GAAP measures to audited statements, and check whether cash conversion supports reported earnings.

## 8. Portfolio construction and risk
Define the objective, time horizon, liquidity needs, constraints and ability to tolerate loss before choosing assets. Diversification reduces some concentration risks but cannot eliminate market-wide losses. Asset allocation, position sizing, rebalancing, costs, taxes and currency exposure all affect outcomes. Understand correlation changes during crises. Leverage and concentrated positions amplify risk.

Useful concepts: volatility, drawdown, beta, correlation, standard deviation, Sharpe ratio, value-at-risk limitations, duration, convexity, liquidity, counterparty exposure and scenario analysis. Historical returns are not promises.

## 9. Market structure and global exchanges
Markets differ in trading hours, holidays, tick sizes, lot sizes, auction sessions, settlement cycles, short-sale rules, circuit breakers, disclosure standards and investor protections. Learn the official rules and regulator for the specific exchange and country. A ticker symbol alone may not uniquely identify a security; confirm exchange, currency, share class and identifier (such as ISIN where used).

## 10. Investor protection and fraud checks
Be cautious of guaranteed returns, urgency, secret strategies, unsolicited messages, fake celebrity endorsements, manipulated screenshots, pump-and-dump groups, impersonated brokers, withdrawal fees and requests to transfer funds to personal wallets. Independently verify the intermediary with the relevant official regulator. Read product documents and fee schedules. Never share passwords, one-time codes or recovery phrases.

## 11. Research workflow
1. Identify the instrument, issuer, exchange, currency and jurisdiction.
2. Find primary disclosures and official product documents.
3. Read business/asset fundamentals and risk factors.
4. Check debt, cash flows, valuation assumptions, fees and liquidity.
5. Compare competitors or appropriate benchmarks.
6. Record the date, source, assumptions and unresolved questions.
7. Separate facts from forecasts and personal opinion.
8. Re-check current filings and rules before acting.

## 12. Data freshness
Company filings, historical prices, index constituents and educational explanations are different data types. Store market datasets with provider, instrument identifier, exchange, currency, timestamp, timezone, frequency, adjustment method, licence and retrieval date. Mark delayed or stale data visibly. Never label an old snapshot as live.

## 13. Jurisdiction matters
Securities laws, investor compensation, tax, reporting, insider-trading rules, short selling, margin, derivatives access and crypto regulation vary by country and can change. A US regulator's guidance is not worldwide law. Use local official regulators and exchange rulebooks for the relevant jurisdiction.

## Glossary
**Bid:** highest displayed buying price. **Ask/offer:** lowest displayed selling price. **Spread:** ask minus bid. **Liquidity:** ability to trade without large price impact. **Volatility:** variability of returns. **Dividend:** distribution declared by a company. **Dilution:** reduction in an existing holder's ownership percentage. **IPO:** first public sale/listing of a company's shares. **Index:** rules-based measure of a market or strategy. **Benchmark:** comparison standard. **Custody:** safekeeping/recordkeeping of assets. **Settlement:** completion of delivery and payment.

## Learning exercises
- Compare two companies using their latest annual reports; identify five differences in business risk.
- Calculate market capitalisation and enterprise value from a fictional balance sheet.
- Explain why a bond's price may fall when yields rise.
- Compare an ETF's expense ratio, holdings, spread and tracking difference.
- Build a paper portfolio and record thesis, risk, benchmark and review date without using real money.
