# Human Biology and Health: A Global Primer

**Coverage:** partial introductory guide. This is general education, not diagnosis or personal medical advice. Health guidance, medicine names and access vary by country; use local licensed health services for individual care.

**Search terms:** human body, anatomy, physiology, health, medicine, medical science, organs, disease, illness, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, nutrition, sleep, mental health, first aid, biology.

## How the body is organised
The body is built from cells, which form tissues, organs and organ systems. Major systems include nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, urinary, immune/lymphatic, musculoskeletal, integumentary and reproductive systems. They interact; a symptom can involve more than one system.

## Core physiology
- **Homeostasis** keeps internal conditions within workable ranges through feedback systems.
- The heart and blood vessels transport oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products.
- The lungs exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide.
- The digestive system breaks food into absorbable nutrients; the liver processes many substances.
- The kidneys regulate fluid, electrolytes and waste excretion.
- The nervous and endocrine systems coordinate rapid and slower regulation.
- The immune system recognises threats and helps defend against infection; immune responses can also cause allergy or autoimmune disease.

## Health, disease and evidence
Health is more than absence of disease. Disease risk reflects genetics, age, environment, behaviour, social conditions and chance. A test result is interpreted with symptoms, history, test accuracy and baseline risk; no test is perfect. Screening is intended for people without symptoms and is not the same as diagnostic testing.

Evidence-based care combines best available research, clinical expertise and the person's circumstances and preferences. Study designs differ in reliability; systematic reviews and well-conducted trials can help, but quality and applicability still matter. Anecdotes and testimonials cannot establish effectiveness.

## Prevention and everyday health
Foundational public-health measures include clean water, sanitation, vaccination, safe food, ventilation, injury prevention and access to care. Sleep, movement, balanced nutrition, supportive relationships and avoiding tobacco reduce many risks. No single diet, supplement or routine suits everyone.

## Medicines and treatment literacy
Medicines have intended effects, possible adverse effects, interactions, contraindications and dosing instructions. Use products according to their official label and a qualified professional's advice. Do not share prescription medicines, combine treatments casually, or stop essential treatment solely because symptoms improve. Antibiotics treat susceptible bacterial infections, not viral infections; unnecessary use contributes to antimicrobial resistance.

## Mental health
Mental health includes emotional, psychological and social wellbeing. Conditions such as depression, anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder, psychosis, trauma-related disorders and substance-use disorders are real health conditions, not character flaws. Support may include psychotherapy, social interventions, medication or combinations. Seek urgent local help if someone may imminently harm themselves or another person.

## First-aid and emergency principles
- Make the scene safe before approaching.
- Contact local emergency services for severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, stroke signs, major bleeding, severe allergic reaction, loss of consciousness, poisoning, serious burns or major injury.
- Follow dispatcher instructions. CPR and rescue-breathing training from a recognised local provider is preferable to relying on text alone.
- Do not induce vomiting after poisoning unless poison-control professionals instruct you to.
- First aid is temporary support, not a replacement for medical assessment.

## Global differences and sources
Health systems, emergency numbers, drug names, vaccine schedules and clinical guidelines differ by jurisdiction and change over time. Consult national public-health agencies, the World Health Organization, recognised medical colleges and local licensed clinicians. Check publication dates and whether a recommendation applies to the relevant age group and region.
