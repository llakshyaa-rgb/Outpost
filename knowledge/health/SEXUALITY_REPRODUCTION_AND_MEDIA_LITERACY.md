# Human Sexuality, Reproduction and Sexual Media Literacy

**Coverage:** introductory, factual and health-focused. This is educational material, not explicit sexual content or individual medical advice. Laws, healthcare access and terminology vary worldwide.

## 1. Puberty and development
Puberty is a developmental process driven by hormonal changes. Timing and sequence vary. Common changes include growth, body hair, skin changes, changes in body composition and development of reproductive organs. Menstruation may begin for people with a uterus; erections and ejaculation may begin for people with testes. Development is not identical for everyone. Questions or distress about development can be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional.

## 2. Reproductive anatomy and function
The reproductive system includes internal and external structures whose anatomy varies. In a typical female reproductive system, ovaries release eggs, fallopian tubes can carry an egg, the uterus can support pregnancy, and the vagina connects the cervix to the outside. In a typical male reproductive system, testes produce sperm and hormones; ducts and glands contribute to semen; the penis can deliver semen. Intersex variations mean some people are born with sex characteristics that do not fit typical textbook categories. Anatomy does not determine a person's worth or identity.

## 3. Menstrual cycle
The menstrual cycle is coordinated by hormones and involves changes in the ovaries and uterine lining. Menstruation is the shedding of the uterine lining when pregnancy has not occurred. Cycle length and symptoms vary, especially during the first years after menstruation begins and around menopause. Very heavy bleeding, severe pain, fainting or sudden major changes deserve medical assessment.

## 4. Reproduction, conception and pregnancy
Pregnancy can begin when sperm fertilises an egg and the resulting embryo implants in the uterus. Ovulation timing varies and calendar estimates are not reliable contraception. Pregnancy involves major physiological changes and requires appropriate prenatal care. Pregnancy outcomes and access to care differ across countries. Infertility is a medical condition with many possible causes and can affect any sex; evaluation and treatment depend on circumstances and local services. Assisted reproduction includes approaches such as insemination and in-vitro fertilisation (IVF).

## 5. Contraception and safer-sex health
Contraceptive methods include barrier methods, hormonal methods, intrauterine devices, implants, fertility-awareness methods and permanent procedures. They differ in effectiveness, reversibility, side effects, suitability and availability. Condoms can reduce the risk of many sexually transmitted infections as well as pregnancy when used correctly; most other contraceptive methods do not prevent STIs. Emergency contraception is time-sensitive and options vary by location. A qualified clinician or pharmacist can explain locally available choices confidentially where the law permits.

## 6. Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)
STIs can be caused by bacteria, viruses or parasites. Some infections cause symptoms; many can be asymptomatic. Testing is the only way to know for many infections. Prevention can include condoms, vaccination where available, testing, treatment, and honest communication with partners. Untreated infections can sometimes cause serious complications. Avoid stigma: an infection is a health issue, not a measure of someone's character.

## 7. Consent, boundaries and relationships
Consent is a freely given, informed, specific and reversible agreement. It cannot be obtained through force, threats, manipulation or exploitation; a person who is asleep, unconscious or too impaired to decide cannot give meaningful consent. Silence or lack of resistance is not consent. Age-of-consent rules and safeguarding laws vary by jurisdiction; check current local law and seek trusted adult or professional support when safety is uncertain. Healthy relationships respect boundaries, privacy, equality and the right to say no.

## 8. Sexual orientation, gender and respectful language
Sexual orientation concerns patterns of romantic, emotional or sexual attraction. Gender identity is a person's internal sense of gender; gender expression is how a person presents themselves. Sex characteristics include anatomy, hormones and chromosomes, which show natural variation. These concepts are related but not interchangeable. Use respectful language and do not assume identity from appearance.

## 9. Nudity: context, privacy and law
Nudity means being unclothed, but its meaning depends on context: medical care, changing, family or cultural practice, art, sport, public spaces and private life have different norms. Non-sexual nudity is not automatically sexual. Laws about public nudity, indecent exposure, photography and age-related safeguarding vary widely. Never photograph, share or publish an identifiable person's intimate image without their explicit permission and a lawful basis.

## 10. Pornography and sexual media literacy
Pornography (often shortened to **porn**) is sexually explicit media made primarily for sexual stimulation. It is media, not a reliable guide to real relationships, bodies, consent, communication or health. It can use performance, editing, scripts and unrealistic portrayals. Laws restrict access by age in many places. Important literacy questions include: Was everyone involved an adult? Was participation freely agreed? Was the material lawfully produced and distributed? Is it staged or edited? Does it portray safe, respectful behaviour? Non-consensual intimate imagery, hidden-camera content, coercion and sexual exploitation are serious harms and may be crimes. Do not seek, save or share sexual images of minors; report suspected exploitation through appropriate local safeguarding channels.

## 11. Online safety and help
Do not send intimate images because of pressure, threats or promises. Be cautious of requests for secrecy, blackmail, grooming, impersonation and attempts to move conversations to private channels. If someone threatens to share an intimate image, preserve relevant evidence without redistributing the image, stop engaging if safe, report the account/platform, and seek help from a trusted adult, local child-protection service or law enforcement. Immediate danger warrants local emergency help.

## 12. What needs local or professional guidance
Symptoms, contraception choices, pregnancy concerns, STI exposure, assault, coercion and mental-health distress deserve confidential, qualified support. Emergency contraception and post-exposure care can be time-sensitive. Do not delay urgent care while searching an offline library.
